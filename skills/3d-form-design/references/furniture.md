# Furniture: sizes, anatomy and how to model it

Scope: common furniture modelled for real-time 3D, so it reads as made rather than assembled from boxes. Sizes in
mm (inch sources converted at 25.4). Sources checked 2026-10-19; most figures come from compiled woodworking design
references (woodbin.com, the "W-" pages below), retailer specifications and trade guides, not from the standards
themselves (BS EN 527, BIFMA X5.1 and Neufert were not read). *practice* marks unsourced working values.

- W-chairs https://woodbin.com/ref/furniture-design/chairs/
- W-tables https://woodbin.com/ref/furniture-design/tables/
- W-desks https://woodbin.com/ref/furniture-design/desks/
- W-kitchen https://woodbin.com/ref/furniture-design/kitchen-cabinets/
- W-shelves https://woodbin.com/?p=1129
- W-beds https://woodbin.com/ref/furniture-design/beds/

## 1. The mistakes to check for first

1. A slab top flush with its legs: no overhang, no apron, no set-back.
2. Legs untapered, or tapered on every face (a table's taper is on the inside faces only).
3. A chair seat square in plan, flat, and at exactly 90 deg to the back.
4. One bevel width on every part, the floor contact edges included.
5. Doors and drawers as one flush block with no 2-3 mm gaps.
6. No plinth or toe recess on casework.
7. A shelf spanning over 800 mm with no divider, edge strip or sag.
8. Cushions as bevelled boxes: no crown, sag, seams or piping.
9. A lamp shade the wrong size for its base.
10. A bed frame the same size as its mattress.
11. Seat and table heights that do not leave 250-330 mm between seat and top.

(Synthesis of the sources below; each item is a sourced rule broken.)

## 3. Anatomy that makes it read as real

**Tables and desks**
- Legs taper on the **inside faces only**, starting about 25 mm below the apron; the outside faces stay plumb, which
  gives the table its stance (https://sawmillcreek.org/goto/post?id=1068285 ,
  https://www.finewoodworking.com/forum/dining-table-leg-taper-question). Examples: 38 to 19 mm over a 760 mm leg.
- The apron sits a few mm back from the legs' outer faces (*practice*), under the top, which overhangs it.
- Edge profiles change the read: an under-bevel makes a thick top look thinner, a bullnose or roundover thicker;
  asymmetric profiles are common (a 12 mm roundover on top, 3 mm under)
  (https://www.finewoodworking.com/2007/01/01/the-right-edge-for-your-tabletop).
- Mid-century legs splay 5-10 deg on angled plates
  (https://tablelegs.com/mccobb-mid-century-modern-coffee-table).

**Chairs and stools**
- Rake (lean seen from the side) and splay (seen from the front) are 5-15 deg; front legs splay more, back legs rake
  more (https://blog.lostartpress.com/2025/09/18/introduction-to-leg-angles/).
- Back legs usually run up into the back posts and bend back above the seat; stretchers (H or box) about 100-200 mm
  off the floor (*practice*).
- A wooden seat is dished (a saddle of about 15-25 mm, *practice*) or upholstered; the seat is a trapezoid.

**Casework**
- The 2-3 mm gaps between fronts are the strongest single cue; a flush box with no gaps reads as a solid block.
  Model the gap as a recess (or the fronts as separate pieces), at least 2 px wide at the usual distance or widened
  to it.
- Plinth or toe kick set back about 75 mm, 100-150 mm tall: the shadow line at the floor (W-kitchen).
- Tops overhang the fronts by about 25 mm (W-kitchen). Bookcases show a back panel and a front edge strip.

**Upholstery**
- Boxed cushions: top and bottom plates sewn to side boxing, piped at the seams
  (https://www.woodcraft.com/blogs/cabinetry-furniture-making/making-box-cushions); a wrap gives a crowned,
  overfilled look (https://www.theupholsteryforum.com/viewtopic.php?p=12196).
- Wrinkles run from the weight towards the corners; the centre is crowned; seats sag in the middle and roll at the
  front edge; back cushions lean on the frame (https://forums.cgarchitect.com/topic/38228-realistic-sofa-cushions-with-max ;
  sag *practice*).

## 4. Technique per piece

| Piece | Build | Geometry vs texture at a few metres |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Table, desk | Top as a board with its edge profile swept or bevelled by tier; aprons as boards set back; legs tapered inside or turned | Model overhang, apron set-back, taper; grain in texture |
| Chair | Side profile first (back leg and post as one bent part), seat as a trapezoid with a dish or cushion, back curved in plan | Model rake, splay, back curve; joints only where they show |
| Turned legs, lamp bases, finials | Revolve a profile, 12-16 segments (*practice*; chord rule for close views) | Silhouette is the whole point: geometry |
| Sofa, armchair, cushions | Cloth with pressure on a flat blank with a seam loop, frame as collider, then retopologise or decimate and bake the wrinkles (https://www.blenderguru.com/posts/couch-william); or sculpt. Piping a 6-sided tube about 4-8 mm swept along the seam (*practice*) | Model crown, sag, seams and piping; weave and small wrinkles in the bake |
| Casework | Carcass boards at real thickness, fronts as separate pieces with gaps, plinth recess, handles | Model gaps (widened to 2 px), plinth, overlap; panel mouldings under about 3 mm in the normal map |
| Shelving | Real shelf thickness, edge strips, a divider within the span, a slight sag on long spans | Books and objects as separate props |

In VR, model anything with real depth: normal-map tricks for depth "can fail badly" in a headset but hold on broad
flat surfaces (https://polycount.com/discussion/185509/bevel-or-use-normal-maps-in-vr ,
https://resources.turbosquid.com/training/games-vr/perils-geo-baking-vr/).

Triangle budgets have no authority; starting ranges (*practice*, from marketplace and forum figures such as a
game-ready armchair at 3,452 triangles,
https://superhivemarket.com/products/vintage-leather-armchair--game-ready-3d-model): dining chair 500-2k, armchair
2-5k, sofa 3-8k, table 300-1.5k, casework 500-3k, lamp 500-2k. The world's budget overrides them.
